# About myself
I am a comparative politics researcher completing my PhD at LMU Munich under Prof. Dr. Nicole Bolleyer. My research looks at small states and self-governing territories: cases that are often left out of standard cross-national datasets, which means they're also largely left out of what we know about democratic transition, breakdown, and institutional change.

Part of my work is theoretical. I'm interested in how the dynamics of small polities differ from those of larger states. For instance, my dissertation argues that small democracies rarely break down because their compact size produces tightly embedded, highly visible elite and social networks that enforce norms informally, even where formal institutions are weak. The other part is empirical infrastructure: testing arguments like this properly requires data that simply hasn't existed, so I've built a self-governance dataset that extends standard statehood criteria to include dozens of additional cases, along with a harmonised global GDP dataset that reconciles major existing sources and adds original data for states missing from all of them. Including these cases doesn't just add sample size; it changes some conclusions the existing literature has treated as settled.

Methodologically, I work mainly with large-N quantitative analysis, but I don't see this as separate from qualitative work. My master's research on small states in WTO dispute settlement was based on expert interviews, and I think of fieldwork and formal data analysis as tools for answering different parts of the same question.

I also have five years of undergraduate teaching experience in comparative politics, including three courses I designed from the ground up, from structure through assessment.

Before my PhD, I completed my BA, Honours, and MA in Political Science and International Relations at the University of Canterbury in New Zealand, along with a Graduate Diploma in Economics from Massey University.
